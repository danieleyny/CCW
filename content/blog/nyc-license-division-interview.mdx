The NYPD License Division interview is a scheduled meeting, part of the investigation into your handgun-license application, where the License Division reviews your file with you and asks about what's in it. It typically happens after you've filed, alongside fingerprinting and the background investigation, within a process that [the NYPD License Division](https://www.nyc.gov/site/nypd/services/law-enforcement/pistol-license.page) describes as taking roughly six months from a complete submission to the decision. This guide covers when it happens, what to bring, and how to prepare — the honest way.

## Where the interview sits in the process

The interview isn't the first step; it's part of the investigation that follows your filing. Per the [NYPD License Division](https://www.nyc.gov/site/nypd/services/law-enforcement/pistol-license.page), roughly six months is typical from a complete submission to the decision letter, and that window covers the interview, fingerprinting, the FBI background check, and the character investigation. The interview is one thread in that braid — the point at which an investigator sits with your file and talks to you directly.

Understanding that placement changes how you prepare. By the time you're interviewed, you've already submitted your references, cohabitant affidavits, training certificate, social-media list, safe photos, and disclosures. The interview isn't a chance to build a new case; it's a conversation about the file you already handed in. Your job is to be consistent with it and candid about it.

## What to bring and expect

Bring identification and any documents the License Division's scheduling instruction tells you to bring — follow that instruction exactly rather than a general checklist, because it's specific to your case. Beyond that, come having actually re-read your own application. The single most common self-inflicted problem is an applicant who can't speak clearly to something they themselves wrote months earlier.

Expect questions that track your file: your background, your reasons for applying, the people and facts in your application. This is a professional interview conducted by an investigator, not an interrogation designed to trip you up — but it is part of a formal investigation, and the [NYPD retains full investigative discretion over the decision](https://www.nyc.gov/site/nypd/services/law-enforcement/pistol-license.page). Treat it with the seriousness that implies.

Because this is your application, you attend and answer for yourself. A consulting service can help you prepare and make sure your file is complete and consistent, but only a New York-licensed attorney may represent an applicant before the License Division — no service speaks for you in that room.

## How to prepare honestly

The whole posture of a strong application is candor, and the interview is where that posture pays off or falls apart. Prepare along these lines:

- **Re-read your entire application first.** Every reference, every disclosure, every date. You want to recognize your own file instantly, not squint at it.
- **Be ready to speak plainly about your history.** If your application discloses an arrest — including a sealed or dismissed one, which [is still disclosed on a New York firearms application](https://www.nyc.gov/site/nypd/about/about-nypd/rules.page) — be prepared to talk about it in your own words. The candor-maximizing move is to own what you disclosed, not to minimize or dodge it. Our guide on a [sealed or dismissed arrest](/sealed-dismissed-arrest-nyc-gun-license) covers that posture in depth.
- **Consistency over polish.** You're not being graded on eloquence. You're being assessed on whether the person in the room matches the file on the desk. Answer honestly and let your answers line up with what you submitted.
- **Don't guess or embellish.** If you don't know something, say so. Inventing a tidy answer to fill a gap is exactly the kind of thing that creates a credibility problem where none existed.

## The line you should not cross

Preparing for the interview is smart. Scripting yourself to shade the truth is not — and it's the fastest way to turn a clean file into a candor problem. Everything about this process is designed to reward disclosure and punish concealment. If the honest answer to a question is uncomfortable, the honest answer is still the right one; the sealed-arrest that's disclosed and explained is a non-event, while the one that's hidden and discovered is a serious one.

There's a specific place this matters: what your record *means* for your application is a legal question, and it's one you may want answered before you walk in. A guide can tell you the general rule; it cannot advise you on your specific arrest, conviction, or history — doing so would be the practice of law. If your case has any real complexity, the time to talk to a [New York-licensed attorney](/do-i-need-a-lawyer) is before the interview, not after.

## Practical logistics

A few concrete things smooth the day:

- **Follow the scheduling instruction to the letter.** Time, place, and what to bring come from the License Division for your specific case. That instruction outranks any general advice, including this page.
- **Give yourself margin.** Arrive early. A rushed, flustered arrival is a bad way to start a formal interview.
- **Keep copies of everything.** Have your own copies of what you submitted so you can reference your file without relying on memory.
- **Mind the training clock.** Because the whole process runs about six months, watch the dates on time-sensitive pieces of your file so nothing lapses while you wait; our [timeline guide](/timeline) walks through the moving parts.

Borough scheduling and local practice vary; if you're applying in the Bronx, our [Bronx gun license guide](/gun-license/bronx) covers that picture. But the substance is the same everywhere: the interview is a conversation about a file you already submitted, the standard is candor, and the honest answer is always the one to give.
