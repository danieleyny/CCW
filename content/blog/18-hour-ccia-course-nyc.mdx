New York's Concealed Carry Improvement Act requires **18 hours of training** before you can be licensed to carry: **16 hours of classroom instruction plus 2 hours of live-fire**, with a state-approved instructor, and a **written test passed at 80% or higher**. That's the rule, straight from [New York State's CCIA (DCJS)](https://www.criminaljustice.ny.gov/). This guide breaks down what the 18 hours consist of, what the test involves, and the one clock that trips people up — the six-month window between finishing and filing.

## The 18 hours, broken down

The headline number is 18, but it's two different things stacked together. Per [New York State (CCIA) · DCJS](https://www.criminaljustice.ny.gov/), the requirement is 16 hours of classroom instruction plus 2 hours of live-fire, delivered by a state-approved instructor, capped by a written test you must pass at 80% or higher.

**The 16 classroom hours** are the bulk of it. This is the law-and-safety portion: the rules that govern owning and carrying a handgun in New York, safe handling and storage, the situations in which use of force is and isn't lawful, and the responsibilities that come with a license. It's substantial precisely because carrying a handgun is a serious legal responsibility, and the state wants licensees who understand the framework they're operating in.

**The 2 live-fire hours** are the range portion — demonstrating safe, competent handling of a handgun in person. It's a smaller slice of the total, but it's not optional or interchangeable with more classroom time. Both components are required; you can't substitute one for the other.

**The written test** closes it out. You have to pass at 80% or higher. It's not a formality — it's a genuine check that the classroom hours landed. The upside is that a course taught well and taken seriously prepares you for it directly; the material on the test is the material you're taught.

## Who can teach it

The training only counts if it's delivered by a **state-approved instructor**. This matters more than it sounds. A range afternoon with a knowledgeable friend, or a course that isn't approved, doesn't satisfy the CCIA requirement no matter how good the instruction is. Before you pay for and complete a course, confirm the instructor is approved to deliver the CCIA curriculum — otherwise you can do all 18 hours and still not have what the application needs.

The training certificate is yours to obtain and include. ## The six-month clock that trips people up

Here's the detail that quietly derails timelines: your training certificate has a shelf life for filing purposes. Per [New York State (CCIA)](https://www.criminaljustice.ny.gov/), **your training certificate must be dated within 6 months of when you file.**

Read that carefully, because the failure mode is subtle. It's not enough to have *done* the training. The certificate has to be dated within six months of your filing. If you complete your 18 hours, then spend eight months gathering references, coordinating cohabitant affidavits, and assembling the rest of your file, your training certificate may have gone stale by the time you're ready to submit — and a stale certificate is a real problem, not a paperwork nicety.

The practical lesson is about **sequencing**. Don't rush to knock out the training first and then let the rest of the application drift. Line up the slower-moving pieces — notarized references, household affidavits, the social-media list — and time your training so the certificate is fresh when you file. For a renewal, the same logic applies to your refreshed live-fire certificate: it needs to be current relative to the renewal. ## How to plan it right

Putting it together:

- **Verify the instructor is state-approved before you book.** This is the one check that, if you skip it, can waste the entire 18 hours.
- **Take the classroom portion seriously.** The 16 hours aren't a hoop — they're the material the 80% test comes from, and the framework you'll be expected to understand at your [License Division interview](/nyc-license-division-interview).
- **Don't front-load the training.** Because the certificate must be dated within six months of filing, do your training in view of when you'll actually submit — not the moment you decide to apply.
- **Sequence around the slow pieces.** References and affidavits take real calendar time. Get those moving, then place your training so the certificate lands fresh.
- **Keep the certificate with your file.** Store it with the rest of your materials so it's ready and its date is easy to check when you assemble the package.

## The bottom line

The training requirement is one of the most concrete, checkable parts of the whole process: 18 hours, split 16 classroom and 2 live-fire, a state-approved instructor, an 80% test — and a certificate that has to be dated within six months of filing. Get an approved instructor, learn the material genuinely, and time it so the certificate is fresh when you submit.
